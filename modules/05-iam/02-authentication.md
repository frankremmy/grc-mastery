# Authentication

> Module 05, lesson 2. Proving you are who you claim to be — and the several
> different ways a system can be convinced.

## Summary

Authentication is the process of verifying the identity of a user, a machine or a
device. Entering a username and password into Gmail or Facebook triggers exactly
that: the system checks the credential and concludes that, since only you should
hold this password, it must be you.

There's more sophistication around it than the password itself. Systems also watch
context — you usually sign in from a particular country and a particular computer.
Log in from somewhere unexpected and you'll be asked for something extra that only
you should know: a date of birth, a pet's name, a security question. All of that
is still authentication.

Three things dominate the topic:

**Passwords.** Still in wide use, not disappearing soon, and a constant feature of
the job.

**Multi-factor authentication.** The system requires more than one thing before
accepting you. Where a username and password used to be the whole transaction,
now it's commonly followed by a code sent to a phone, sent to another email
address, or generated by an authenticator app.

**Biometrics.** Using something biologically unique — a fingerprint, a retina
scan — to establish identity. Common for building access and increasingly common
generally.

## The factors underneath

The examples above sort into three categories, which is the scaffolding that makes
MFA meaningful:

| Factor | Means | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Something you **know** | A secret held in memory | Password, PIN, security question, date of birth |
| Something you **have** | A physical item in your possession | Phone receiving a code, authenticator app, hardware token, smart card |
| Something you **are** | A biological characteristic | Fingerprint, retina or iris scan, face recognition |

The distinction matters because multi-factor means factors from **different**
categories. A password plus a security question is two things you know — two
locks, one key type, and both defeated by the same kind of attack. A password plus
a code from an authenticator app is genuinely two factors.

Context — location, device, time — is a fourth signal, usually described as
risk-based or adaptive authentication. It isn't a factor in its own right; it's
what decides whether to ask for another one. The extra prompt when you sign in
from a new country is step-up authentication.

## Key concepts

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authentication | Verifying a claimed identity — user, machine or device | The gate everything else depends on |
| Credential | The thing presented as proof | Its strength sets the ceiling for the whole control |
| Risk-based authentication | Using context to decide how much proof to demand | Keeps friction low for normal behaviour, high for unusual behaviour |
| Step-up authentication | Asking for more when something looks off | The extra question after an unfamiliar login |
| Knowledge-based verification | Security questions, date of birth, memorable details | Convenient, and the weakest of the knowledge factors |
| Biometrics | Authentication by biological characteristic | Hard to forge, and impossible to reissue if compromised |
| Machine and device identity | Systems authenticate too, not only people | Often authenticated by secrets that never expire |

## Where this shows up in a real job

Password resets and account recovery are daily support work, and they've taught me
something the lesson doesn't reach: the recovery path is where accounts are
actually lost. Nobody brute-forces a strong password. They persuade a support agent,
or they answer a security question with information from a public profile, or they
take over the email account the reset link goes to. The front door is usually fine.

WordPress makes the factor question concrete. Sites run 2FA plugins that send a
code by email — which sounds like multi-factor and often isn't, because the mailbox
is protected by a password and reachable from the same compromised session. The
intent is right; the implementation collapses two factors into one.

Building Entra ID in the lab gave me a working sense of conditional access — the
policy layer that decides when to demand more. That's configuration familiarity,
not experience of what those policies do to a workforce of thousands who all need
to sign in on a Monday morning.

## My take

The factor taxonomy is the thing to hold, because it turns a list of examples into
a test you can apply. Faced with any authentication scheme, ask which categories
are actually represented. A lot of what gets marketed as multi-factor turns out to
be one factor wearing two coats — password plus security question, password plus
an emailed code where the email is reachable with the same password.

Two things:

**Authentication is capped by enrolment and recovery, and both sit outside the
login screen.** Verifying a credential only proves someone holds the credential. It
says nothing about whether the right person was given it in the first place, or
whether the process for replacing a forgotten one is as strong as the credential
itself. An organisation with a rigorous password policy and a helpdesk that resets
passwords on a phone call has the security of the phone call. This is the weakest
link principle from the OWASP lesson, and in identity the weak link is almost
always recovery.

**Biometrics have a property nothing else has: they can't be reissued.** A leaked
password is changed in a minute. A compromised fingerprint is compromised
permanently — you have ten, and none can be revoked. Well-designed systems avoid
this by keeping the biometric local, using it to unlock a credential stored on the
device rather than sending it anywhere, so what crosses the network is a key rather
than a fingerprint. That design detail is what makes phone unlock reasonable and a
central database of raw biometric templates a very different proposition.

**Security questions are the weakest knowledge factor and are often treated as a
strong one.** The answers are frequently discoverable — a date of birth from public
records, a pet's name or a mother's maiden name from social media — and unlike a
password they can't be changed once exposed. As a step-up challenge after an
unusual login they're better than nothing. As the thing standing between an
attacker and an account reset, they're a soft target, which is where a GRC
assessment should look.
