# Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

> Module 05, lesson 3. The control closest to non-negotiable — and the two ways
> organisations still get it wrong.

## Summary

MFA reduces the likelihood of unauthorised access by requiring more than a
password. An attacker who obtains the password still can't get in.

The types map onto the three factor categories. **Something you know** — a second
password, a PIN, a security question such as a mother's maiden name or a favourite
city. **Something you have** — a physical token, a separate key, or a temporary
code generated by an authenticator app on your phone. **Something you are** —
biometrics: retina, fingerprint, facial recognition, now familiar from unlocking
an iPhone by face.

The practical advice is direct: enable it everywhere, personally as well as
professionally. It works. Not impossible to defeat, but significantly harder.

**Tooling doesn't matter much.** Okta was among the pioneers; there's Google
Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, and plenty besides, all doing more or less
the same thing. What matters far more than the product choice is **buy-in from
senior management**. That used to be a hard conversation — as recently as a couple
of years ago it meant persuading executives that MFA was worth the hassle to
users — and it's now largely expected.

For a GRC professional doing an assessment, finding an organisation without MFA
should produce one of the first and strongest recommendations you make. The
lesson's position is that it's non-negotiable.

Then two problems that persist even where MFA exists.

### Problem one: exemptions

Exemptions are requests to be excused from MFA, and they need watching closely.

Some are convenience — CEOs, CFOs and senior executives who simply can't be
bothered. Some are genuine: board directors often have no corporate account at all
and use external email addresses, which makes enforcement awkward. And some are
disputes about the device — staff who won't install an authenticator on a personal
phone unless the company provides one.

Convincing individuals isn't the GRC professional's job. What is the job: find out
**how many exemptions exist**, and present the risk clearly. Executives are
targeted far more heavily with phishing and scams than ordinary employees, because
the payoff is larger. An exempt CEO is a substantially easier target than a
protected junior employee, which inverts the risk exactly where you least want it
inverted.

You may not succeed in getting MFA enforced universally. Your job is to raise it
as a risk, state the severity honestly, and record it. The lesson's experience is
that organisations have been grateful for this — and that an external consultant
is often heard where the internal security team making the same argument was not.

### Problem two: MFA treated as invincibility

Some IT managers, executives and inexperienced security practitioners assume MFA
removes the need for other measures. The example given: an organisation declining
penetration testing on the grounds that it had MFA and therefore couldn't be
hacked.

MFA makes attacks harder. There are many ways around it. Having MFA is one layer —
the defence in depth principle from the OWASP lesson. It's comparable to a
firewall: you can't run an organisation without one, and having one doesn't make
you unhackable. It reduces the likelihood and impact of certain attacks, not all
of them.

The broader caution: you will encounter people in security, IT management and
network engineering whose knowledge is thinner than you expect. Have these
conversations early in an assessment rather than discovering the assumption late.

## Key concepts

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MFA | Requiring more than one factor before granting access | The highest-value single control in most assessments |
| Factor categories | Know / have / are | Genuine MFA draws from different categories |
| Tool neutrality | Okta, Google, Microsoft and others are broadly equivalent | Don't let product selection delay the decision |
| Executive buy-in | Leadership accepting the friction on behalf of the organisation | The real blocker, historically and sometimes still |
| Exemptions | Users excused from the requirement | The gap that quietly reopens the risk MFA closed |
| Executive targeting | Senior people attract disproportionate phishing | Makes exemptions for executives the worst possible ones |
| The consultant effect | External voices heard where internal ones weren't | Useful to know, and slightly uncomfortable |
| MFA is a layer, not a shield | One control among many, bypassable | Defence in depth applied to identity |

## Where this shows up in a real job

The personal-device objection is the one I've seen most. Asking someone to install
a work authenticator on a phone they paid for is a real ask, and dismissing it as
obstruction doesn't resolve it. The organisations that handle it well have an
answer ready — a hardware token for anyone who wants one — rather than an argument.

The invincibility problem has a WordPress version. Sites install a security plugin
and treat the question as settled: no backups tested, no update discipline, weak
hosting-level access. The plugin is real and does something; it's the belief that
it's sufficient that causes the damage.

I'd also note that my own MFA experience is as a user and a lab builder, not as
someone who has run a rollout. Configuring conditional access in Entra ID is not
the same as getting several hundred people through an enrolment deadline, and the
hard part of MFA is clearly the second thing.

## My take

The strongest idea here is that **an exemption register is the real MFA
deliverable.** Whether an organisation "has MFA" is close to a meaningless
question — nearly all say yes. The assessable questions are: who is excluded, why,
who approved it, when it gets reviewed, and what compensates in the meantime. That
turns an unwinnable argument about a specific executive into a documented list
with names and dates, which is far harder to leave alone.

Three things:

**Exemptions concentrate in exactly the wrong place, and that's the argument to
make.** The people most able to secure an exemption are the most heavily targeted
and hold the most access. The organisation ends up with its strongest control
protecting everyone except the accounts worth attacking. Framed that way, it's a
sharper conversation than a general appeal to policy consistency — and it's a risk
statement rather than a complaint, which is the GRC contribution.

**The genuine cases need a designed answer, not an exception.** Board members on
external email addresses is a real constraint, and "excuse them" isn't the only
option — a separate account for board material, a hardware token, or a secure board
portal all address it. The distinction worth drawing in an assessment is between an
exemption because the problem is hard and an exemption because nobody designed for
it. The first is a risk accepted knowingly; the second is a gap nobody has worked
on yet.

**Not all MFA is equally strong, and the invincibility problem is partly that
nobody says so.** SMS codes can be intercepted or defeated by SIM swap; push
notifications are vulnerable to fatigue attacks where a user eventually approves
one to make it stop; codes can be relayed through a convincing phishing proxy in
real time. Hardware keys resist these because the credential is bound to the site
itself. So "do you have MFA" is the wrong question; "which method, on which
systems, and does it resist phishing" is the assessment question. That also gives a
better answer to the executive who thinks MFA makes them unhackable — not "you're
wrong to trust it", but "here's specifically how this method is defeated."

One more thing to sit with: the observation that an external consultant gets heard
where the internal team wasn't. That's worth remembering in both directions. It's
useful leverage when consulting — and if I end up on the inside, it means my
argument failing isn't necessarily an argument problem, and bringing in an outside
voice may be the tactic rather than an admission of defeat.
