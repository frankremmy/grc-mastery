# Cyber security frameworks

> Module 02, lesson 5. A short bridge: where the controls we recommend actually
> come from.

## Summary

Having assessed a risk, the GRC professional recommends controls: to remove the
risk, or to reduce its likelihood or its impact. The point of this lesson is that
choosing those controls isn't improvisation. You don't invent a measure because it
sounds sensible in the room.

Controls come from frameworks: published industry standards, built on research,
tested over time and across a lot of organisations. That provenance is what makes
a recommendation defensible. "We should encrypt the backups" is an opinion; "this
maps to control X in standard Y, which we're aligned to" is a position that
survives a challenge from someone who outranks you, and that an auditor can check.

The lesson is a signpost rather than a topic — it's telling me the next several
lessons are the frameworks themselves.

## Key concepts

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Framework | A published, researched standard for security controls or practice | Gives a recommendation a source outside the recommender's judgement |
| Control selection | Choosing treatments from an established catalogue rather than inventing them | Makes the choice reviewable, comparable and auditable |
| Time-tested | Refined across many organisations over years | The reason a framework carries weight in a room where you might not |

## Where this shows up in a real job

This is what the ISO 27001 work at Helium Health actually consisted of, though I
wouldn't have described it this way at the time. The controls weren't dreamed up;
they came out of Annex A, and the work was deciding which applied to us and
writing down why. Selecting from a catalogue and justifying the selection *is* the
activity — I just hadn't connected it to the phrase "control framework".

## My take

The value of a framework isn't that it knows your organisation — it doesn't. It's
that it moves the argument off your personal credibility. As someone pivoting into
this field without a security title yet, that matters more to me than it would to
a CISO: the framework is doing the persuading, and I'm doing the mapping.

The thing I want to stay conscious of is what a framework does and doesn't answer.
It tells you what a good control set looks like in general. It doesn't tell you
what your biggest problem is, or what to do first; that still comes out of the
risk assessment. Working the other way round, picking controls off a list and
calling the result a risk programme, is the failure mode I'd most easily fall into
because it feels productive and produces a lot of paper.

Also worth sorting as they arrive: "framework" is going to cover several different
kinds of thing in this course. A control catalogue, a set of design principles, a
model for thinking about security properties and a programme structure aren't
interchangeable, even though all four get called frameworks. Knowing which one I'm
holding tells me what question it can answer.
