# What most organisations get wrong about cyber security risk

> Module 02, lesson 10. The funding trap, and the argument you have to be able to
> make to get out of it.

## Summary

The module closes on why risk management goes wrong in practice, and the answer
isn't ignorance — it's pressure. Security teams are underfunded, overworked, and
hiring into a skills shortage. The day goes on firefighting: configuring
firewalls, stopping attacks, working through penetration test findings.

What gets dropped is the assessment and the documentation, because those are the
tasks with no alarm attached. And that creates the trap. A team that hasn't
documented its risks can't show decision makers what the organisation is exposed
to. Decision makers who can't see the exposure don't release funding. Without
funding the team stays underwater, and the documentation stays undone. Lack of
funding causes the gap in documentation, and the gap in documentation sustains the
lack of funding.

The second problem is a gap in appreciation. Plenty of technically skilled
people — penetration testers, analysts, forensic investigators — regard risk
management as boring, as a documentation exercise, as time that could have been
spent on real work. They don't see what it could do for them.

The response the lesson asks for is empathy plus communication. Being able to
explain the value of risk management clearly and consistently — to technical
colleagues, and to finance, senior management and the board, who are busy and need
it succinct.

## Key concepts

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resource pressure | Underfunding, overwork and a skills shortage acting together | The conditions risk management actually has to survive, not an excuse |
| Firefighting crowds out assessment | Urgent operational work displaces work with no deadline | Explains why the gap appears in capable teams, not just weak ones |
| The funding cycle | No documentation → no visibility → no funding → no capacity to document | The trap is self-sustaining; breaking it takes a deliberate act |
| Undocumented exposure | Risk that exists but has never been written down or reported | Invisible to the people who could fund it, so effectively it doesn't exist to them |
| Appreciation gap | Technical practitioners seeing risk work as bureaucracy | A real obstacle to doing the job, and one you meet from inside your own team |
| Audience-appropriate communication | Same message, sized and framed for technical peers or the board | The core skill the whole module has been building toward |

## Where this shows up in a real job

I've been on the technical side of this. When the queue is deep, documentation is
the first thing to go — not from disagreement with it, but because nothing breaks
today if it doesn't get done. I understand the instinct the lesson describes
because I've had it.

That's the useful position to be in, moving into GRC. The colleagues who find risk
registers tedious aren't a foreign audience to me; I've sat with them and mostly
agreed. What I can bring is the argument in terms they'd accept — that the
register is how the work they're already doing turns into headcount and budget,
rather than a parallel exercise competing with it.

The support parallel holds again: a recurring problem nobody writes up stays an
individual's private burden, and gets solved from scratch every time. Writing it
up is how it becomes something the organisation acts on.

## My take

The vicious cycle is the most useful idea in the module, because it inverts the
priority. If you can only do one thing with limited time, the intuitive answer is
to fix something, and the answer the cycle suggests is to document and report. The
fix helps one system. The documented, reported risk is the only thing that
changes next year's budget. Documenting is also the cheapest activity available to
a team with no money — which makes it exactly the right move for the team that
feels least able to spare the time.

On the appreciation gap, I'd start from the assumption that the sceptics are
reasoning from experience. Most of them have seen a register that went into a
drawer and changed nothing. That's not a failure of understanding, it's an
accurate read of a register that didn't work. The persuasive answer isn't
explaining the theory again — it's being able to point at a risk that got written
up and came back as funding, headcount or a decision someone signed. One concrete
instance beats any amount of advocacy.

Which sets the bar for what I should be producing: registers that lead to a
decision. If the thing I write doesn't move money, change a priority or get
signed by someone accountable, the sceptics were right about that particular
document.
