# Lesson 26 — Preparing for the audit (and the audit plan)

## Summary

Once an audit is confirmed as feasible, preparation has four steps:

1. **Review documented information.** For example: scope, policies, objectives, the risk assessment and treatment plan, the SoA, procedures, and previous findings and corrective actions. The point of the review is to find audit trails, high-risk areas, the evidence needed, who to interview and how to sample. A weak review leads to weak findings.
2. **Prepare the audit plan.** It should be risk-based, practical and flexible.
3. **Assign team work.** Base it on competence, technical knowledge, independence, how significant each area is, and efficient use of time.
4. **Prepare working documents.** Each checklist item should state the criterion, the activity, the expected evidence and the sampling technique. Working documents guide the auditor; they must never stop them from following an audit trail.

The course walks through an example Stage 2 plan for a fictional cloud payroll company, CloudHarbour. The instructor says this format is what the final assessment tests, so every section needs to be present and written out explicitly.

## Key concepts — the audit plan sections

| Section | What it must contain | Typical junior mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Audit details | Organisation, audit type, date, duration, location, team | — |
| Objectives | Conformity, effective implementation, enough evidence for the conclusion or recommendation | Objectives that are vague or missing |
| Scope and exclusions | What's in and out, taken from the ISMS scope statement | Scope not matching the ISMS scope, or exclusions missing |
| Criteria | Clauses 4–10, Annex A controls (numbers checked against the SoA), internal documents, legal and contractual requirements | Wrong control numbers |
| Team responsibilities | Who covers which clauses and controls, and who runs the opening and closing meetings and approves the report | — |
| Risk-based priorities | Where extra attention goes, taken from the risk assessment, treatment plan and past findings | A "risk-based" plan that never cites the risks |
| Schedule | Time, activity, criteria, auditor, auditee representative, method and location | No auditee representative named |
| Sampling | Method, population, rationale, and when to expand the sample | Sampling done but never declared |
| Audit risks and responses | Risks to the audit itself and how each is handled | Section left out |
| Communication and reporting | Primary contact, escalation, how potential findings are raised, how plan changes are recorded, confidentiality, report timing, who makes the certification decision | — |

## Where this shows up in a real job

- When I build the Stark audit plan, I'll take the priorities straight from the Stark risk register's high-rated risks and from my clause 6–10 critiques. That gives each priority a traceable reason.
- In my own working documents, each checklist row will have five columns: criterion, question, expected evidence, sample, and result. That is the evidence tracker from earlier with an audit column added.

## Where I'd push back on the example plan

The plan is a useful template. Reviewing it as a lead auditor, I would still raise five things:

1. **The criteria are too narrow for a Stage 2.** Only three Annex A controls are listed (A.8.3, A.8.13, A.5.19). A Stage 2 audit evaluates whether the *whole* ISMS is implemented. The criteria should be "Annex A controls determined as applicable in the SoA", and those three should be named as *priority* controls. As written, the certification recommendation would rest on three controls.
2. **One day is very likely too short.** The audit time a certification body plans is driven by IAF MD 5 (headcount, complexity, sites). A cloud payroll provider with support staff would normally need several days for Stage 2. In this plan the lead auditor covers clauses 4–10 in about 2.5 hours. The "activities exceed available time" risk is already happening, not hypothetical.
3. **The sampling rationale contradicts itself.** It says judgement-based sampling will produce "representative evidence". Judgement samples are chosen to target risk, so they are deliberately *not* representative and their results can't be extrapolated. Under the fair-presentation principle, the plan should say that.
4. **There is no link to Stage 1.** A Stage 2 plan should cite the Stage 1 report and any areas of concern it raised. Those are the first thing Stage 2 has to verify.
5. **The legal requirements row cites clauses 4.2 and 6.1 but not A.5.31,** the control that covers legal, statutory, regulatory and contractual requirements. A.5.31 is the obvious criterion for that session.

What the plan gets right, and is worth copying:
- conditions for expanding the sample;
- a private audit-team review before the closing meeting;
- potential findings are discussed for factual accuracy;
- changes to the plan are recorded with reasons;
- an explicit statement that the certification decision is made independently by the certification body.

## My take

The plan is where the principles become specific. Risk-based shows up as the priorities section. Evidence-based shows up as declared sampling. Fair presentation shows up as written-down audit risks. Independence shows up as assigned roles and the separate certification decision. If a principle has no section in the plan, the audit probably won't apply it. For the assessment, I'll use the course template's section order and correct the five weaknesses above.
