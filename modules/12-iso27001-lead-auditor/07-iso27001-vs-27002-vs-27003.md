# ISO 27001 vs 27002 vs 27003 vs Annex A

> Module 12, lesson 7. _Which document does what, and which one you can actually
> be certified against._

## Summary

The ISO 27000 family contains several related standards covering different parts
of building and running an information security programme. Confusing them is
common and avoidable.

| Standard | Role | Certifiable? |
| --- | --- | :---: |
| **ISO 27000** | Vocabulary, definitions and foundational concepts for the whole family — risk, asset, control, incident | No |
| **ISO 27001** | The requirements standard. Defines what an ISMS must do | **Yes** |
| **ISO 27002** | Guidance on *how* to implement the Annex A controls — objectives, practical advice, examples | No |
| **ISO 27003** | Guidance on *how to build the ISMS project* — objectives, management buy-in, project structure, risk assessment | No |
| **Annex A** | The reference list of 93 controls inside ISO 27001 | Part of 27001 |

The one-line versions:

- **27000** defines the language
- **27001** tells you what you must do to be certified
- **27002** shows how you might implement the controls
- **27003** guides you through building the whole management system
- **Annex A** is the menu you select from, based on your risk assessment

### Common mistakes

- Thinking Annex A is the whole standard. It's one part of ISO 27001
- Implementing all of Annex A by default instead of selecting by risk
- Assuming ISO 27002 is mandatory. It's guidance and not required for
  certification

## Worth adding

**Only ISO 27001 is certifiable, and that's a usable test.** If a supplier sends
you an "ISO 27002 certificate", something is wrong — either they've
misunderstood their own paperwork or someone sold them a document. The same
applies to claims of being "ISO 27000 compliant". Neither standard has
requirements to conform to. It's a cheap, fast credibility check in third-party
assessment, and it turns up on CVs and LinkedIn profiles too.

**27002 is where Annex A actually comes from.** Annex A in ISO 27001 gives you
control titles and brief statements — it's essentially an index. ISO 27002 holds
the full text: the purpose of each control, implementation guidance, and the
attributes (control type, information security properties, cybersecurity
concepts). The four themes and 93 controls were restructured in **ISO 27002
first, published February 2022**, with ISO 27001 following that October. So the
elaboration flows from 27002 down into Annex A, not the other way around.

**This is where lesson 5's "should" matters.** ISO 27002 is written almost
entirely in *should*, because it's a recommendation document. That's precisely
why it isn't mandatory — the verbal form tells you its status. The requirement
lives in 27001's *shall*; 27002 offers one way to satisfy it.

**The family is bigger than four documents**, and several members map directly
onto modules from this course:

| Standard | Covers | Relevant module |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **27005** | Information security risk management | 02 risk management |
| **27017** | Cloud services security | 07 data security |
| **27018** | Protecting PII in public clouds | 07 data security |
| **27035** | Information security incident management | 08 incident response |
| **27036** | Supplier relationship security | 09 TPRM |
| **27701** | Privacy information management — extends 27001 to privacy | GDPR work |

**ISO 27005 is a notable omission from this lesson.** It's the
information-security-specific risk management standard, and it's arguably more
relevant to ISO 27001 work than 27003 is. The module covers ISO 31000 instead
(lesson 17), which is the *generic* risk management standard — Monash's published
summary cites 31000 alignment, so both are used in practice. Worth knowing 27005
exists and is the narrower, security-specific one.

**ISO 27701 is the one I'd pay attention to for my own market.** It extends an
ISO 27001 ISMS into a Privacy Information Management System, it's certifiable as
an extension, and it maps onto GDPR obligations. For EU and Malta-based work,
"27001 plus 27701" is a considerably stronger position than 27001 alone, and it's
the answer to a client who asks whether certification helps with GDPR — 27001 on
its own doesn't, 27701 is designed to.

## My take

The distinction that does real work is **requirements versus guidance**, and it
maps onto the normative/informative lesson exactly. 27001 is what you're audited
against. Everything else in the family exists to help you satisfy it, and none of
it creates obligations.

That has a practical consequence I'd want to get right as an implementer: you can
disagree with ISO 27002. If its guidance for a control doesn't fit the
organisation, you can implement the control differently, and as long as the
control objective is met and you can evidence it, the auditor has nothing to
raise. People treat 27002 as a checklist because it's detailed and reassuring, and
that's how organisations end up implementing guidance they didn't need in order
to satisfy a requirement they could have met another way.

The supplier-assessment angle keeps recurring in this module and it's sharpening
into something concrete. Given a certification claim, the questions are: which
standard (only 27001 certifies), which version (2022 now), which body and is it
accredited, what scope, and what does the SoA say. Five questions, all answerable
from documents a supplier can reasonably provide, and together they tell you far
more than the certificate alone. That's a better version of what I did with
Horizon Labs in module 9.
