# Lesson 23 — Establishing and managing the audit programme

## Summary

An audit programme is the set of arrangements for one or more audits, planned over a defined period for a particular purpose. It relates to individual audits the way a programme of work relates to its projects. The programme sets the overarching objective (for example, reaching certification or improving weak areas), and each audit has its own plan and objectives that feed into it.

- The **audit client** makes sure the programme's objectives are set.
- The **audit programme manager** runs the programme. This person may also act as the audit team leader on some audits, but the two roles are separate.
- The programme has its own risks and opportunities, and the programme manager owns them.

## Key concepts

| | Audit programme | Audit plan |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | Several audits over a period | One audit |
| Owned by | Audit programme manager | Audit team leader (lead auditor) |
| Sets | Objectives, extent, schedule, risks, resources, team competence | Dates, auditees, scope, methods, logistics for that audit |
| Analogy | Programme | Project |

**Programme objectives** can include checking conformity, evaluating effectiveness, verifying corrective actions, evaluating suppliers, and finding improvements. They should take into account context, interested parties, risks, changes, previous results and how the ISMS is performing.

**How much auditing is needed** (number, frequency, duration, coverage) depends on the organisation's size, the ISMS scope, how important each process is, locations, risks, past findings, significant changes and legal or contractual requirements.

**Programme risks** include too little time or resources, auditors who aren't competent, conflicts of interest, unsuitable methods, missing evidence, communication failures, technology or AI failures, and confidentiality breaches.

**Opportunities** include remote auditing, bringing in specialists, data analytics, and combining related audits.

**Context tools.** PESTLE (external factors) and SWOT (internal and external) help decide where audit attention should go.

## Where this shows up in a real job

- **A three-year internal audit programme for a 27001 organisation** is usually a grid of clauses 4–10 and the applicable Annex A themes against years, weighted by risk. Management-system clauses and high-risk controls get audited every year. Stable, low-risk controls get audited once in the cycle. The Stark project should produce one of these.
- **Combined audits** are a real efficiency gain. If the organisation also holds ISO 9001 or ISO 27701, one integrated audit can cover the shared clauses (context, leadership, document control, internal audit, management review).
- **The audit programme is the place to record reasoning,** such as "access management moved to annual after two incidents". That way the certification body can see the programme reacting to results, not just repeating last year's schedule.

## Where I'd push back

- **This isn't optional for an ISMS.** ISO 19011 guidance says "should", but ISO 27001 clause 9.2.2 says the organisation *shall* plan, establish, implement and maintain an audit programme. The programme has to cover frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning requirements and reporting, and it has to take into account how important each process is and the results of previous audits. Without a documented programme, the internal audit is a nonconformity, however good the individual audits were.
- **There are two different programmes.** The organisation's *internal* audit programme comes under clause 9.2. A certification body runs its *own* programme for each client, a three-year cycle under ISO/IEC 17021-1: Stage 1, Stage 2, surveillance audits in years 2 and 3, then recertification. The lesson mixes the two. They have different owners and different rules.
- **Who is the audit client internally?** For internal ISMS audits, the client is normally top management, or a delegated committee such as the audit and risk committee. Naming this person avoids the clause 9 problem I raised for Stark, where the ISM appointed the auditor who audits the ISM's own work.
- **Coverage.** The standard doesn't explicitly require every clause and applicable control to be audited within the cycle. In practice, though, certification bodies expect full coverage over three years, and they raise a finding when areas are never audited.

## My take

The programme sets out why each audit happens, and the plan sets out how. A clause 9.2 finding is far more likely to come from a missing programme, or one that never changes, than from a single badly run audit. An auditor reading the programme should be able to see that last year's findings changed this year's schedule.
