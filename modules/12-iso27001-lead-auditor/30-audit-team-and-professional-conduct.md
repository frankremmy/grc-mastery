# Lesson 30 — Audit team members, professional conduct and reflection

## Summary

An audit can be run by one auditor or by a team. A team may include an audit team leader, auditors, auditors-in-training and technical experts. Guides and observers can help but are not normally team members.

- **The team leader** assigns work, confirms objectives, scope and criteria, monitors progress, coordinates communication, reviews evidence and proposed findings, resolves disagreements, forms conclusions, leads the opening and closing meetings, and completes the report.
- **Auditors** review documents, interview people, observe, collect and verify evidence, sample, and draft findings. They escalate delays, conflicts and contradictory evidence.
- **Technical experts** bring knowledge the team lacks, such as cloud, network, software development, privacy or industry technology. They are not auditors unless they are competent and appointed as one.
- **Auditors-in-training** work under supervision.

Team members raise potential findings with each other before they go to the auditee. Disagreements are settled by looking at the criterion and the evidence. Everyone protects confidential information.

The course provides three example records. I describe them here and don't reproduce them:

- **E1 — Code of conduct and scenario responses.** Five situations and how a lead auditor responds (see the table below).
- **E2 — Lead auditor self-evaluation and development plan.** Rated competent in most areas and "developing" in time management and adaptability. The plan: build contingency time into schedules, take advanced remote-auditing training, and build cloud security knowledge.
- **E3 — Professional reflection.** Reflections on integrity and impartiality, confidentiality, professional communication, evidence-based judgement, and how the lessons apply next time.

## Key concepts — the scenarios

| Scenario | Principle at risk | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Auditee pressures the team to downgrade a nonconformity | Integrity, impartiality | Keep the finding the evidence supports, record the pressure, and escalate to the audit client if it continues |
| Sensitive evidence sent to a personal email | Confidentiality | Stop, move to the approved channel, and handle the copy under the agreed rules |
| Auditor previously worked on the area being audited | Independence | Reassign |
| Auditor is argumentative with the auditee | Professional conduct | Bring the discussion back to criterion and evidence, and coach or reassign |
| Auditee offers an expensive gift before the audit | Integrity, impartiality | Decline, disclose to the firm, record |

## Where this shows up in a real job

- **Competence is maintained as well as achieved.** Certification bodies track auditor CPD and logged audit days. A self-evaluation with a development plan is what that record looks like.
- **My own plan, in the same format:**
  - log audit hours under a lead auditor;
  - build cloud control knowledge (AWS AI Practitioner is in progress, and the CloudHarbour-style SaaS scenarios need it);
  - practise writing findings to the requirement → evidence → gap standard.

## Where I'd push back

- **"Accepting a gift is strictly illegal" overstates it.** A gift is an impartiality threat and almost always breaks the audit firm's code of conduct and the certification body's impartiality rules under ISO/IEC 17021-1. It becomes *bribery*, which is a crime under anti-bribery laws, when it is offered or accepted to influence the result. An expensive gift offered "in anticipation of the audit" plausibly meets that test, so declining is the only safe answer. The general rule, though, is "conflict, decline, disclose", not that every gift is illegal.
- **The personal-email scenario needs two different responses depending on who sent it.** If *our* auditor used personal email, it's a confidentiality breach by the audit firm and should go through the firm's incident process. If the *auditee* sent sensitive data in clear text, it's an information-handling weakness on their side. Raise it with them, and it may be evidence relevant to A.5.14 (information transfer). Either way, don't keep extra copies.
- **Self-evaluation is part of the standard, not just good practice.** ISO 19011 covers evaluating auditor competence and maintaining and improving it (clause 7). Certification bodies also have to monitor auditor performance under 17021-1, usually through periodic witnessed audits. E2 is what that requirement looks like on paper.
- **The lesson text confirms my point from lesson 27.** Guides and observers are "not normally members of the audit team", which matches 19011 and contradicts the earlier transcript.

## My take

This section closes the loop with the seven principles. E1 shows what they look like under pressure, and E2 and E3 show what they look like afterwards. Each audit should leave behind a reflection with at least one development action, so the reflection shows what I learned and not only what went well. That's the same continual-improvement loop as clause 10, applied to myself.
