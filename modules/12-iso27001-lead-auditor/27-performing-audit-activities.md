# Lesson 27 — Starting and performing audit activities

## Summary

Fieldwork has five parts:

1. The opening meeting
2. Ongoing communication
3. Collecting and verifying evidence
4. Sampling
5. Adjusting the plan when reality differs from it

**Opening meeting.** The lead auditor chairs it. It has three purposes:
- confirm the plan is agreed;
- introduce everyone and their roles;
- confirm the audit activities can go ahead.

It also covers scope, methods, sampling, access, confidentiality, how findings will be communicated, and how the closing meeting will work.

**During fieldwork.** The team reviews progress, evidence, potential findings and open audit trails as it goes. Significant changes to the plan are communicated and recorded.

**Evidence.** It must be relevant, reliable and verifiable. Reliability depends on the source, how complete and current the evidence is, whether it is consistent, and whether it can be verified. An interview statement needs supporting evidence wherever such evidence ought to exist. Interview with open questions and follow-ups, and avoid leading or accusatory questions. Observe without disrupting the work.

**Sampling.** Judgement-based sampling picks items by risk or significance. Statistical sampling picks them by a probability method. The risk in both is that the sample doesn't represent the population, so findings have to state that limitation.

The course gives three example records, described here rather than reproduced:
- **Opening meeting record.** Attendees, confirmation of each item in the plan, and sign-off that activities can proceed, from both the lead auditor and the auditee representative.
- **Audit team briefing and work allocation.** Objectives, priority areas, who audits what (the technical auditor takes access and backup, the compliance auditor takes suppliers and legal), changes and concerns, and team confirmation.
- **Team management and finding review record.** Two mid-audit situations, a review of findings, a disagreement log, and the lead auditor's confirmation.

## Key concepts — the mid-audit situations

| Situation | Response in the example | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| The compliance auditor previously worked on the client's supplier process | Reassigned. The lead auditor takes over the supplier review | Independence |
| The client can't produce two of the sampled privileged-access records | Sample expanded, more time allocated to access control | Evidence-based, due care |
| Access not removed within the required timeframe in sampled leavers | Finding **retained** | Evidence-based |
| Competence finding raised from an interview only; training records later showed conformity | Finding **withdrawn** | Evidence-based, fair presentation |
| Auditee disputes the access finding and supplies more logs | Reviewed, finding still retained, disagreement recorded | Integrity |

The instructor's practical lesson: when you ask for more evidence, also ask for more time, and write the request down. More time costs money, and that is exactly why people skip it.

## Where this shows up in a real job

- **Leaver access is the classic finding.** Before fieldwork, compare the HR leavers list with the directory's disable dates. Then the "sample" is the full population, and the auditee can't argue that the sample was unlucky.
- **A record anyone can reconstruct.** The finding-review record lets someone who wasn't there, such as a director or the certification body, see what happened and judge the lead auditor's work. Write it for that reader.

## Where I'd push back

- **Guide and observer are defined differently in ISO 19011.** The transcript calls the observer a member of the audit team, such as an intern. In ISO 19011 an observer *accompanies* the team but isn't part of it. Observers often come from outside the audit team: the auditee, a regulator, the audit client, or an accreditation body witnessing a certification body auditor. A junior shadowing the audit fits the definition only if they aren't acting as an auditor. A guide is appointed by the auditee to help the team. Both must stay out of the evidence.
- **The access finding is probably mapped to the wrong control.** Not removing a leaver's access on time is mainly A.5.18 *Access rights* (provisioning, review, removal), together with A.6.5 *Responsibilities after termination*. A.8.3 *Information access restriction* is about systems enforcing access policy. A misattributed finding gives the auditee an easy way to dispute it. The instructor also warned that wrong control numbers are a common junior mistake.
- **The competence finding shouldn't have been a finding yet.** If it rested only on an interview, it was a *potential* finding or an open audit trail. Withdrawing it was correct. The better habit is to not raise a finding until the supporting evidence has been checked.
- **The impartiality conflict should have been caught earlier.** Conflicts are supposed to be identified during programme setup and audit initiation. Finding one mid-audit is a lesson learned for the programme review. Moving the work to the lead auditor also costs time, so the plan's time risk should be revisited.
- **An unresolved disagreement goes in the report, not just the working papers.** Fair presentation requires the report to record disagreements the auditee didn't accept. In a certification audit, the auditee can also appeal under the certification body's process (ISO/IEC 17021-1).

## My take

These three records are the audit's audit trail. The finding-review record is the one worth getting right. "Retained" and "withdrawn," each with the evidence behind it, shows the team followed the evidence in both directions: it dropped a weak finding as readily as it held a strong one under pushback. A record that only ever retains findings looks like confirmation bias.
