# Education and awareness programs

> Module 06, lesson 4. What a programme is actually made of, from the baseline
> everyone has to the extras most organisations skip.

## Summary

An awareness programme is as much a part of a security programme as a firewall or
anti-malware — and it has to be *effective*, not merely present, if it's going to
reduce the likelihood of phishing and social engineering succeeding.

**The baseline: a web-based training course.** The usual starting point and the
first thing to check when assessing a programme. It isn't trying to make anyone a
security engineer; it teaches employees the basics — complex passwords, don't click
phishing links, don't fall for scams. It must run **periodically**, not once.
Annually is enough for most organisations.

**The next step: simulated phishing.** Using a platform — Mimecast, Proofpoint or
similar — the security team sends a fake phishing email and observes who clicks
and who reports it. It works because training tells people what to look for and the
simulation gives them the practice. Anyone who clicks receives targeted follow-up
training on phishing specifically. Common frequency is every three to six months;
the lesson's view is that quarterly is excessive and **six to twelve months** is
enough, depending on organisation size.

Most organisations stop there — a training module and occasional simulations — and
for most that's sufficient.

**For mature organisations, three cheap additions:**

**Physical posters.** Underrated and inexpensive. Placed in the building or near
offices, showing what a phishing email looks like, carrying reminders about
password complexity, or presenting an infographic with more detail. A constant
passive reminder, and consistently positive in effect.

**Lunch-and-learn or brown bag sessions.** An hour over lunch with an external
speaker, with food provided to encourage attendance, covering phishing or another
security topic. A break from the routine of web courses and simulations, and
generally well received.

**Intranet blog posts.** Most organisations have an internal news system. Writing
articles about phishing, a specific scam, two-factor authentication or malware
costs little, and people engage with articles.

## The programme, layered

| Layer | What it is | Cadence | Maturity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Web-based training | Baseline course on security fundamentals | Annually | Every organisation |
| Simulated phishing | Fake phishing email; click and report rates measured | Every 6–12 months | Most organisations |
| Targeted follow-up | Extra training for those who clicked | On each campaign | With simulations |
| Posters | Passive visual reminders in the workplace | Ongoing, refreshed | Mature |
| Lunch and learn | Live session with an external speaker | Occasional | Mature |
| Intranet articles | Written content on current threats | Ongoing | Mature |

## Key concepts

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline training | Fundamentals for all employees | The first thing an assessment looks for |
| Periodic delivery | Repeated rather than one-off | Awareness decays; a single session isn't a programme |
| Simulated phishing | Controlled fake attack to test and practise | Moves training from knowing to doing |
| Click rate and report rate | Who fell for it, and who flagged it | The two measures a campaign produces |
| Targeted remediation | Follow-up training for those who clicked | Directs effort where it's needed |
| Passive reinforcement | Posters and articles present between formal sessions | Covers the long gaps between training events |
| Varying the format | Live sessions, written content, visual reminders | Different formats reach people the standard course doesn't |

## Where this shows up in a real job

I've been the audience for the baseline course and the target of the simulations,
which gives me a view of the receiving end. The annual module is something people
complete while doing something else; the simulated phish is the part anyone
remembers, because it involves an actual decision rather than a video.

The lunch-and-learn format is the one I'd expect to work best, and it's close to
something I already do — the WordPress meetups and do_action events I ran in Uyo
were exactly this shape: a live session, an audience giving up their time
voluntarily, and content that had to earn attention rather than assume it. That
transfers directly, and it's the part of a programme I could contribute to now
rather than eventually.

I haven't operated a simulation platform or designed a campaign, so my view of
those is from principles and from being on the list.

## My take

The most valuable line in the lesson is that the programme must be **effective**,
not merely present — because almost every organisation can produce evidence of an
annual course, and that evidence answers a question nobody should be asking. The
assessable questions are whether completion is near-universal, whether the content
has changed since it was bought, and whether anything measurable improved. "We run
annual training" is a statement about an activity, not a control.

Three things:

**Report rate deserves more prominence than click rate.** Simulations produce two
numbers, and the lesson focuses on the first — who clicked, who gets remedial
training. The second is the one that matters operationally. Clicking is a mistake
you can reduce but never eliminate; reporting is a capability you can build, and
it's what converts an employee from a liability into a sensor. A campaign where
clicks fall and reports stay flat has taught people to be cautious and passive.
Worth flagging now, because the next lesson is on measurement.

**Follow-up training for clickers carries a risk that depends entirely on framing.**
Targeted remediation is sensible. It becomes counterproductive the moment it reads
as punishment — because the same people then hide real mistakes, and the interval
between a real click and a real report is most of what determines the damage.
Short, non-punitive, and never reported to a manager as a performance matter.

**The cheap additions do a job the formal training can't: they cover the gaps.**
Annual training plus a simulation every six months leaves long stretches with
nothing, and awareness decays across them. Posters, articles and occasional
sessions aren't a lesser version of the course — they're the part that operates on
an ordinary Tuesday, which is when phishing arrives. That's an argument worth
making to a sceptical executive, since posters read as trivial next to a platform
licence and cost almost nothing.

One practical note on assessing a programme rather than describing it: the useful
questions are what proportion completed the training, whether new joiners get it
before they get email access, whether the content reflects attacks the organisation
has actually seen, and what happened to the people who reported something real. The
last one tells you whether the reporting channel works, which no completion
statistic will.
