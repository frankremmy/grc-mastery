# Problems with cyber security audit in the real world

> Module 03, lesson 4. Why auditors get a bad name, and why second-line cyber
> audit is so often weak.

## Summary

Two problems, one about how findings land and one about who is doing the auditing.

**Findings are received badly.** An auditor's job is to look for gaps and then
report them, and plenty of IT managers and security professionals don't take that
well. The lesson attributes this largely to ego — people not enjoying an outsider
telling them their work isn't good enough — and argues findings should be treated
as free external perspective on how to improve.

There's a harder edge to it too. Findings sometimes get used against people: fed
into a negative performance review, or used to force a team to drop everything and
clear the list. So the defensiveness isn't purely imagined; in some organisations
the consequences are real.

**Second-line cyber audit is frequently poor.** This is the bigger structural
problem. Second-line cyber auditors tend to get hired into a general risk
function — one built around financial and accounting risk, with no security
specialism. Meanwhile people who do have security knowledge often don't want the
role: some don't know it exists, and many would rather do hands-on technical work.
The result is organisations hiring people who aren't equipped for it, who then
audit first line without really understanding what they're looking at.

The instructor's view is that this isn't going to resolve soon, and that closing
the gap is part of what the course is for.

## Key concepts

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Findings as criticism | Reports of gaps being read as judgement of the people who own them | Determines whether an audit improves anything or just generates friction |
| Weaponised findings | Audit output used for performance management or to reprioritise a team by force | Where defensiveness stops being irrational |
| The second-line skills gap | Cyber auditors hired into general risk teams without security expertise | Produces audits that miss what matters and challenge what doesn't |
| Career aversion | Technically skilled people avoiding or not knowing about audit roles | Why the gap persists — it's a supply problem, not just a hiring one |
| Unqualified challenge | Someone auditing work they don't understand | Costs the audit function its credibility with the teams it reviews |

## Where this shows up in a real job

The certification run at Helium Health had some of the second dynamic in it —
findings arriving, and a scramble to close them against a date. What made that
stressful wasn't the findings themselves; it was that they landed at a point where
there was no room to do anything but react.

From the support side, I recognise the first problem in a smaller form. Feedback
about work lands very differently depending on whether the person giving it
understands the constraints you were working under. A reviewer who knows why a
shortcut was taken and still thinks it was wrong is worth listening to. One who
doesn't know is easy to dismiss — fairly or not.

I've not been an auditor, so I'm reading this as someone likely to be on the
receiving end for a while yet, and eventually on the delivering end.

## My take

I'd put less weight on ego than the lesson does, because the structural
explanations are better and they point at things you can actually change.

A finding usually lands on a team that already knew. Module 2 closed on the
vicious cycle: underfunded, overworked teams that don't get to do the assessment
and documentation work, and therefore can't show decision makers what's wrong, and
therefore stay underfunded. An auditor walks into that and documents exactly the
gaps the team has been unable to get funded. Being told you're failing at
something you've been asking for resources to fix is genuinely irritating, and
that reaction isn't vanity.

Which suggests the reframe I want to hold onto: **a good finding is the team's
best funding argument.** The same gap, written by the team, is a request that
competes with everything else. Written by an independent auditor and reported to
the board, it's an obligation with a name attached. Auditors who present findings
that way — here is the evidence you needed to make your case — get cooperation.
Those who present them as a scorecard get a defensive room and a list that gets
closed on paper.

And if findings *are* used in performance reviews, defensiveness is the correct
response, not a character flaw. Fix the incentive before complaining about the
behaviour: an audit function whose output can harm the people it reviews will be
managed rather than helped, every time.

On the second-line skills gap, two things:

**It's an opportunity, and I should be honest that this is the gap I'm walking
into.** The shortage described is specifically people who understand security and
are willing to do assurance work. That's the combination I'm building, and it's a
better argument for the pivot than enthusiasm is.

**It's also a warning about where to take a first role.** A second-line function
with nobody experienced in it is a place where I'd learn slowly and pick up bad
habits with no one to correct them. Given a choice, joining a team that already
does this well is worth more than a better title somewhere it doesn't exist yet.

The credibility point cuts both ways too. An auditor who doesn't understand what
they're reviewing gets dismissed by first line, and often deserves to be — which
is precisely why the technical grounding matters. Being able to ask for the right
evidence, and recognise a bad answer when you get one, is what buys the standing
to make a finding stick.
